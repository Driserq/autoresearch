## Q T1 (trivial) — toilet paper vs paper towel to blow your nose?

### Answer A
Toilet paper — but the gap is ~a penny per blow, so absolutely it barely matters. Cost/sheet (2026 US name-brand): Charmin ≈ $0.005; Bounty Select-A-Size ≈ $0.025–0.03 (store/warehouse brands push TP ~0.2¢, paper towel ~0.6–1¢). Per blow: TP 3 squares ≈ 1.5¢; paper towel 1 Select-A-Size sheet ≈ 2.8¢. Verdict: TP cheaper by ~1.3¢ (paper towel ~85–90% more); negligible absolute. A week-long cold (~280 blows) ≈ $4.20 TP vs ~$7.80 paper towel (~$3.60 total). Caveat: per square inch they nearly converge; if you tear a half sheet costs almost tie. Real deciders are comfort/skin-friendliness. Sources: topsourcehygiene; Walmart Charmin; Home Depot Cottonelle; Amazon Bounty; Hip2Save.

### Answer B
Toilet paper is cheaper — but by less than the per-sheet gap suggests; for a single blow the two are roughly a wash (both ~0.5–1.5¢), TP usually edging it. Per sheet: budget TP ~0.18–0.25¢ (Kirkland), mid-tier ~0.3–0.5¢; paper towel ~1–2¢ (5–10× more). But per blow: TP takes ~3–4 squares → ~0.6–0.8¢ budget (~1–1.2¢ premium); one paper-towel half-sheet (3× the area, thicker) → ~0.5¢ (Kirkland) to ~1¢ (Bounty). Budget-vs-budget within a fraction of a cent, TP a hair cheaper; premium paper towel → TP wins clearly; you'd need 5+ TP squares before a budget towel wins. Never flush paper towels (clogs — a plumber dwarfs the savings). Cheapest of all: a cloth handkerchief. Sources: calculator.academy; whogivesacrap; Hip2Save; happymoneysaver; baodapaper.

## Q T2 (trivial) — boil 500 ml: electric kettle vs microwave?

### Answer A
Kettle cheaper — but only ~half a cent per boil (~1.0¢ vs ~1.5¢); the bigger lever is not overfilling. Physics: 0.5 kg × 4186 × 80 ≈ 0.047 kWh into the water. Kettle ~80–90% efficient (element submerged) → ~0.055 kWh; microwave ~50–60% → ~0.085 kWh. At ~18¢/kWh (EIA avg 17.9¢; state range 12¢ ND–47¢ HI) → ~1.0¢ vs ~1.5¢. Note: "microwave cheaper per cup" claims are real only if you overfill the kettle; boil the same 500 ml measured and the kettle wins. Microwaved water can superheat (burn risk); kettle also faster (~1.5–2 min vs 2.5–3.5). Money difference ~$3–4/yr. Sources: EIA; Electric Choice; Fellow; Electric Kettles Guide; dchidell measured test.

### Answer B
Kettle is cheaper — but only by about half a cent per boil, trivial in absolute terms. Physics: 0.5 kg × 4186 J/kg·°C × 80 °C = 167,440 J ≈ 0.0465 kWh into the water. Inputs (looked up, US 2026): electricity 18.83¢/kWh (EIA Apr 2026); kettle ~85% efficient; microwave ~55% (a measured test: kettle 327 kJ vs microwave 741 kJ per liter). Math: kettle 0.0547 kWh → $0.0103 (~1.03¢); microwave 0.0846 kWh → $0.0159 (~1.59¢). Verdict: kettle ~0.56¢ cheaper (~35%). Scale: ~180 boils to save $1; ~$6/yr at 3/day. The biggest lever is how much water you heat, not the appliance. State range 12–47¢ scales both but not the ~35% gap. Sources: EIA EPM; dchidell test; electrickettlesguide.

## Q T3 (trivial) — dry hands at home: paper towels vs small electric dryer?

### Answer A
Electric hand dryer cheaper per dry (~0.06¢) vs paper towels (~1.5¢) — ~95% less, but ~1.4¢ is negligible per dry. Inputs (US 2026): electricity ~$0.18/kWh (EIA); dryer ~1000 W, ~12 s (range 500–1800 W, 8–15 s); paper towel ~$0.005–0.016/sheet, 1–2 sheets. Dryer 0.0033 kWh × $0.18 ≈ $0.0006. At household volume (~10/day, ~3,650/yr): paper towels ~$55/yr vs dryer ~$2/yr → ~$50–55/yr saved. Amortize appliance ($80–150 / 7 yr = $12–20/yr) → all-in ~$15–22/yr, payback ~2 years. Sources: EIA; electricchoice; electricrates; Hip2Save; Amazon/handdryer.com.

### Answer B
Per dry the electric dryer is much cheaper to run, but at home paper towels can be "cheaper" for a while because of the dryer's upfront cost. Dryer: 1000–1800 W, ~10–15 s at ~17–18¢/kWh → ~0.06¢ (1000 W/12 s) to ~0.14¢ (1500 W/20 s); even in Hawaii <0.5¢. Paper towels ~1–2¢/sheet, 1–2 sheets → ~1–3¢/dry (10–30× the dryer). But a home dryer costs ~$50–120 upfront (paper towels ~$0). Saving ~1.5¢/dry, an ~$80 dryer needs ~5,000 dries → ~1 yr (busy household) to ~2 yr (2-person). Honest caveats: a reusable cloth hand towel beats both; the dryer only saves if you actually use it (loud, may not fully dry in 15 s). Sources: electricrates; EIA; Excel Dryer FAQ; handdryersandmore; Hip2Save; Amazon.

Bottled base case: bulk store-brand $1.30/gal (near ~$1.22 avg; deliberately steelmanning — name brand/single-serve $2.50–5+/gal) → ~$475/yr. Pitcher: 9.1 filters × $6 ($54.75) + tap $3.65 + pitcher $30/5yr ($6) ≈ $65/yr (~$90 yr1). Difference ~$410/yr (~86%). Sensitivity: bottled is the swing variable and was chosen bottled-favorable — even cheapest bulk (~$1.05) is ~6×; name-brand $900–1,800/yr (93–96% cheaper); verdict never reverses. Filter count driven by the 40-gal rating (9), not the 2-month rule (6). Not a lead purifier unless Elite. Sources: IBWA; sypshydration; Amazon/Brita specs; Walmart; lawnlove; EPA.

## Q M3 (mid) — paper towels vs reusable "Swedish" dishcloths over a year?

### Answer A
Cloths clearly cheaper — save ~$60–150+/yr, ahead within a month or two. Paper towels (Bounty) ~$1.50–2.40/roll; moderate household ~1.5–2 rolls/2 weeks (~40–50/yr) → ~$100–200 (heavy users $400+). Swedish cloths ~$0.60–2 each bulk (premium $4–6); last 6–12 mo light / 1–2 mo heavy → ~4–12/yr → ~$10–40 + negligible laundry. "One cloth replaces 15–17 rolls" = marketing. Honest caveats: hygiene needs rinse/air-dry/wash or they smell; paper towels still win for grease/raw meat/pet messes → most go hybrid; savings scale with habits ($50–70 light user, $150+ heavy). Sources: zerowastestore; girocompany; Yahoo; Hip2Save; thetakeout; stasher; Home Depot/Costco Bounty; earth911.

### Answer B
**Cloths cheaper ~$65/yr (~65%)** — smaller than the "replaces 17 rolls" marketing once you count rotation/replacement. Paper towels: ~1.5 standard rolls/week ≈ 80/yr (Statista); bulk ~$1.25/standard-roll-equiv → ~$100/yr (range $75 all-bulk–$160 convenience). Dishcloths: 10-pack ~$16–20 (~$2 each, often $6–10 on sale); under daily duty keep ~4–6 in rotation, each ~2–3 mo → ~10/yr → ~$20 cloths + ~$15 washing ≈ $35/yr (range $15–90). Verdict: ~$100 vs ~$35. Even pessimistic reusable (~$90) ≈ cheapest all-bulk paper towel (~$75) → worst case ~break-even. Biggest swings: your usage + cloth lifespan (used a harsher ~2–3 mo vs marketing); most households stay hybrid. Sources: Statista; ecofamilylife; BJs/Target/Quill; Amazon/Hip2Save; zerowastestore; CNN Underscored.

## Q H1 (high) — lease vs buy a $30k car over 5 years (12k mi/yr)?

### Answer A
**Buy is cheaper by ~$4,000 (16%): ~$21,700 vs lease ~$25,900.** APR ~7.0% (Bankrate 6.96%, Edmunds 6.9%); 5-yr depreciation ~42% (iSeeCars) → ~$17,400 residual; competitive effective lease ~$400/mo (scaled from Corolla ~$329, Civic ~$341; market avg $619 skewed by SUVs). BUY: down $3,000 + 60 pmts $32,080 (int ~$5,080) + maint ~$4,000 − resale $17,400 ≈ $21,700 (and you own it). LEASE (36-mo + 24-mo to span 5 yr): $14,400 + $10,320 + maint $1,200 − $0 ≈ $25,900. Driver: residual recapture + steepest-depreciation years + acquisition/disposition fees twice. Sensitivity: fast-depreciating EV/luxury (~55–60%) erases most of the gap; subsidized promo leases (~$300–320/mo) can flip it; credit tier matters; hold 8–10 yr → buying by a landslide. Sources: Bankrate; USNews; iSeeCars; LendingTree; TrueCar; thevantagegroup.

### Answer B
**Buy is cheaper — by ~$4,000–7,000 — because you own an asset at the end.** APR ~6.9% (Bankrate); $0 down → ~$593/mo × 60 = ~$35,600 (int ~$5,600); 5-yr value ~55–60% depreciation → resale ~$12,000–13,000 (KBB/Carfax). Net buy ≈ $35,600 − $12,600 ≈ $23,000, loan then gone. Lease ~$400/mo (Edmunds $30k example ~$407; avg $619 across pricier cars) chained 36+24 mo → ~$26,000–27,000 incl drive-offs/disposition, $0 equity. Side-by-side table → net buy ~$23,000 vs lease ~$26,500. 12k mi/yr avoids overage. Taxes tilt slightly to lease (tax on payments not full price); maintenance/warranty tilt to lease years 4–5 (~$1–2k), not enough to flip. Consumer Reports agrees. Buy for a 5-yr hold; a 1–2-yr-old used car beats leasing on every count. Sources: Bankrate; Edmunds; KBB; Carfax; Consumer Reports.

## Q H2 (high) — repair vs replace a 7-yr-old fridge with a failed compressor?

### Answer A
Standard/mid-range fridge → usually **replace** (but close); high-end/built-in → **repair**. Compressor $700–1,250 (up to $1,500 full sealed-system): parts $200–400, labor $500–850. New avg ~$1,500 (basic $600–1,000; std $600–2,300; built-in $2,250–10,450). 50% rule table by type: basic ~$850 new → repair 80–100%+ (replace); std ~$1,500 → ~50–80% (usually replace); high-end $2,500+ → 10–40% (repair). At 7 yr you're under the 10-yr line, mid-life. THREE things first: (1) confirm it's really the compressor — failed relays/boards/fans mimic it and cost $100–400; pay the ~$150–200 diagnostic; (2) check LG "linear/inverter" compressors 2014–2022 — Rosen v. LG settlement gives a 10-yr parts+labor warranty, repair could be free + ~$450 cash; (3) a repaired sealed system carries second-failure risk. Energy savings only ~$15–30/yr (overstated for a young fridge). Sources: HomeGuide; Angi; Reviewed; HWA; Expo Appliance; ENERGY STAR; Consumer Reports (LG settlement); ClassAction.org.

### Answer B
**Lean toward replacing — but check your warranty first, that's what flips it.** Compressor replacement $700–1,250 (~$1,000 mid; parts $100–800, labor+service $500–850); new comparable $1,000–2,000 (~$1,500) + $150–200 install; lifespan ~12 yr (DOE); new-unit energy savings only ~$15–25/yr vs a 2019 unit. 50% rule: half of $1,500 = $750; a ~$1,000 compressor is ~67% → over the line. Amortize: repair ~$1,000 / ~5 remaining yr = ~$200/yr; replace ~$1,675 / 12 yr − $20 energy ≈ ~$120/yr → replacement wins. Flips to REPAIR if compressor still under warranty (labor-only $500–850) or a written quote under ~$750 on an otherwise-sound fridge; if you'd only replace with an $800–900 unit, threshold drops to ~$425 → repair almost never. Honest caveats: energy savings not decisive (true only for pre-2010); ranges wide — get a written quote + confirm warranty; built-in/specialty shift it. Sources: Angi; HomeGuide; HomeAdvisor; energy.gov; ENERGY STAR; Town Appliance; Reviewed.

## Q H3 (high) — electric vs natural-gas clothes dryer over its lifetime?

### Answer A
Have a gas hookup already → **gas wins ~$850–900 lifetime (~35–40%)**, break-even <1 yr; need a new gas line → essentially a **wash**. Inputs (US avg 2026): electricity $0.18/kWh, gas ~$1.55/therm (EIA); electric ~3.3 kWh/load, gas ~0.20 therm + ~0.2 kWh/load; 300 loads/yr; life 13 yr; gas premium ~$75; new line ~$525–1,250 (mid ~$850). Per load: electric $0.59 vs gas $0.35 → save $0.24 (41%). Annual: $178 vs $105 → $73/yr; 13 yr $2,300 vs $1,365 → ~$950 operating. Net: have gas → $950 − $75 = ~$875 (break-even ~13 mo); install line → $950 − $925 ≈ +$25 (break-even ~yr 12.5, coin flip). Heat-pump electric (~1.6 kWh/load ≈ $0.29) nearly matches gas with no line. Regional prices swing it hard. Sources: EIA×2; michaelbluejay; omnicalculator; appliancerunningcost; Angi.

### Answer B
If your home already has both a gas hookup and a 240 V outlet, a gas dryer is usually cheaper over its life — ~$500–900 across 10–13 yr — but that flips in three cases (new gas line, cheap-electric region, or a heat-pump comparison). Electricity ~17.9¢/kWh, gas ~$1.50/therm (EIA). Electric ~3 kWh/load → $0.50–0.55; gas ~0.2 therm + ~0.4 kWh → $0.30–0.35 (30–50% cheaper, ~$50–80/yr); heat-pump ~1.5 kWh → $0.25–0.30. Table across all three. Gas premium $50–100 → payback 1–2 yr if hookups exist. The catch: no 240 V outlet adds $300–800; no gas line adds $500–1,500 (can wipe the savings → electric cheaper total). Local price ratio and heat-pump dryers can flip it. Check your own ¢/kWh and $/therm. Sources: EIA×2; aboutdarwin; Angi; baltimorechronicle; homeguide; directenergy; dryera; jla.
